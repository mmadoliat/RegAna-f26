Statistical Computing and Data Analysis

The answers below follow the question numbering in hw-2.qmd. Numerical results use the 32-car Table B.3 data and the simple linear regression of mileage (y) on engine displacement (x1). The printed values are rounded; R retains full precision for calculations.

The fitted equation is

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{MPG}} = 33.7227 - 0.04736\,\mathrm{Displacement}. \]

The estimated slope means that an increase of one cubic inch in engine displacement is associated with a decrease of about 0.0474 miles per gallon in predicted mileage, on average within the range of these data. The intercept predicts 33.72 mpg at zero displacement. A zero-displacement engine is outside the observed range and not practically meaningful, so the intercept should not be interpreted as the fuel economy of an actual automobile.

2. Confidence intervals for \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\), and \(\sigma^2\)

For \(j=0,1\), a two-sided 95% confidence interval is

\[ b_j \pm t_{0.975,n-2}\operatorname{SE}(b_j). \]

confint(fit, level = .95)
                  2.5 %      97.5 %
(Intercept) 30.77383383 36.67151954
x1          -0.05694883 -0.03777032

Thus, approximately,

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_0 &: (30.7738,\ 36.6715),\\ \beta_1 &: (-0.05695,\ -0.03777). \end{aligned} \]

Under the normal-error assumption, \(SSE/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-2}\). Hence

\[ \boxed{\left( \frac{(n-2)MSE}{\chi^2_{0.975,n-2}}, \frac{(n-2)MSE}{\chi^2_{0.025,n-2}} \right)} \]

is a 95% confidence interval for \(\sigma^2\).

sigma2_ci <- c(
  lower = df * MSE / qchisq(.975, df = df),
  upper = df * MSE / qchisq(.025, df = df)
)
c(n = n, df = df, SSE = SSE, MSE = MSE)
         n         df        SSE        MSE 
 32.000000  30.000000 281.824378   9.394146 
sigma2_ci
    lower     upper 
 5.998913 16.784480

The variance interval is approximately \((5.9989,\ 16.7845)\) mpg\(^2\). This is an interval for the error variance, not for the standard deviation \(\sigma\).

3. Test the slope

\[ H_0:\beta_1=0,\qquad H_1:\beta_1\ne0. \]

Under \(H_0\), the statistic \(t=b_1/\operatorname{SE}(b_1)\) has a \(t_{n-2}\) distribution. Reject \(H_0\) when \(|t|>t_{0.975,30}\) or equivalently when \(p<0.05\).

slope_row <- summary(fit)$coefficients["x1", ]
slope_row
     Estimate    Std. Error       t value      Pr(>|t|) 
-4.735958e-02  4.695385e-03 -1.008641e+01  3.743041e-11 
t_critical <- qt(.975, df = df)
c(critical_value = t_critical,
  t_statistic = slope_row["t value"],
  p_value = slope_row["Pr(>|t|)"])
     critical_value t_statistic.t value    p_value.Pr(>|t|) 
       2.042272e+00       -1.008641e+01        3.743041e-11

Here \(t\approx-10.0864\) on 30 degrees of freedom, with \(p\approx 3.743\times10^{-11}\). Reject \(H_0\): displacement has a statistically significant linear association with gasoline mileage. The estimated association is negative.

For significance of regression, test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) against \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0\). The statistic is

\[ F=\frac{MS_R}{MS_{\mathrm{res}}} =\frac{955.7197}{9.3941}\approx101.7357, \]

with \((1,30)\) degrees of freedom and \(p\approx3.743\times10^{-11}\). Reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level: regression on displacement is significant.

F_stat <- SSR / MSE
c(F_statistic = F_stat,
  F_from_t_squared = unname(slope_row["t value"]^2),
  p_value = pf(F_stat, df1 = 1, df2 = df, lower.tail = FALSE))
     F_statistic F_from_t_squared          p_value 
    1.017357e+02     1.017357e+02     3.743041e-11

In simple linear regression with an intercept, \(F=t^2\) for the slope, so the ANOVA \(F\)-test and the two-sided slope \(t\)-test give identical \(p\)-values and decisions.

5. Coefficient of determination

R2 <- summary(fit)$r.squared
c(R_squared = R2, percent_explained = 100 * R2,
  check = SSR / SST)
        R_squared percent_explained             check 
        0.7722712        77.2271242         0.7722712

\(R^2\approx0.7723\), so approximately 77.23% of the observed variation in gasoline mileage is accounted for by its fitted linear relationship with engine displacement. This does not establish that displacement causes the variation.

6. Confidence interval for the mean mileage at 275 in\(^3\)

ci_mean
       fit      lwr      upr
1 20.69879 19.58807 21.80952

The fitted mean mileage is 20.6988 mpg. Its 95% confidence interval is approximately \((19.5881,\ 21.8095)\) mpg. This interval estimates the average mileage for the population of automobiles with a 275 in\(^3\) engine under the fitted model.

For reference, the standard error of the estimated mean at \(x_0=275\) is

\[ \operatorname{SE}\{\hat\mu(x_0)\} =s\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}. \]

7. Prediction for a new automobile at 275 in\(^3\)

(a) The point prediction is \(\hat y(275)=\mathbf{20.6988}\) mpg.

(b) The 95% prediction interval is approximately \((14.3415,\ 27.0561)\) mpg.

pi_car
       fit      lwr      upr
1 20.69879 14.34147 27.05611

(c) The prediction interval is wider than the confidence interval in Question 6 because it must account for both uncertainty in the fitted mean and the variability of an individual automobile around that mean. Specifically,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{SE}\{\hat\mu(x_0)\} &=s\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}},\\ \operatorname{SE}_{\mathrm{pred}}(x_0) &=s\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}. \end{aligned} \]

The additional \(1\) under the second square root represents the variation of a new response around its conditional mean.

9. Numerical verification of least-squares identities

The sums below are approximately zero up to floating-point rounding; in exact arithmetic the corresponding least-squares identities hold exactly for a model with an intercept.

(a) Residuals sum to zero.

sum(e)
[1] -2.803313e-15

The intercept normal equation is \(\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)=\sum_i e_i=0\).

(b) Observed and fitted responses have the same sum.

c(sum_y = sum(y), sum_fitted = sum(yhat), difference = sum(y) - sum(yhat))
     sum_y sum_fitted difference 
    647.14     647.14       0.00

Because \(\sum_i e_i=0\), we have \(\sum_i y_i-\sum_i\hat y_i=0\). Both sums are approximately 647.14.

(c) The fitted line passes through the centroid.

c(xbar = mean(x), ybar = mean(y),
  fitted_at_xbar = unname(predict(fit, newdata = data.frame(x1 = mean(x)))),
  difference = unname(predict(fit, newdata = data.frame(x1 = mean(x)))) - mean(y))
          xbar           ybar fitted_at_xbar     difference 
  2.850437e+02   2.022312e+01   2.022312e+01  -7.105427e-15

The centroid is approximately \((285.0438,\ 20.2231)\), and \(b_0+b_1\bar x=\bar y\) because \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\).

(d) Predictor and residuals are orthogonal.

sum(x * e)
[1] -3.979039e-13

The slope normal equation gives \(\sum_i x_i(y_i-\hat y_i)=0\).

(e) Fitted values and residuals are orthogonal.

sum(yhat * e)
[1] -3.597123e-14

Since \(\hat y_i=b_0+b_1x_i\),

\[ \sum_i\hat y_i e_i =b_0\sum_i e_i+b_1\sum_i x_ie_i=0. \]

Mathematical Derivation and Reasoning

10. Unbiasedness of the intercept estimator

In simple linear regression,

\[ b_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)Y_i}{S_{xx}}, \qquad b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar x, \qquad S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2. \]

As the \(x_i\)’s are fixed and \(E(Y_i)=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\),

\[ \begin{aligned} E(b_1) &=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)E(Y_i)}{S_{xx}}\\ &=\frac{\beta_0\sum_i(x_i-\bar x) +\beta_1\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)x_i}{S_{xx}}\\ &=\frac{0+\beta_1S_{xx}}{S_{xx}} =\beta_1. \end{aligned} \]

Also \(E(\bar Y)=\beta_0+\beta_1\bar x\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{E(b_0)=E(\bar Y)-\bar x\,E(b_1) =(\beta_0+\beta_1\bar x)-\bar x\beta_1=\beta_0.} \]

Thus the least-squares intercept estimator is unbiased. Normality is not required for this expectation calculation; fixed predictors, the correct conditional mean model, and zero-mean errors suffice.

11. ANOVA decomposition

Write \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). Since \(y_i-\bar y=(\hat y_i-\bar y)+e_i\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2 &=\sum_i\bigl[(\hat y_i-\bar y)+e_i\bigr]^2\\ &=\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2 +\sum_i e_i^2 +2\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)e_i. \end{aligned} \]

The cross term is zero because the least-squares normal equations imply \(\sum_i e_i=0\) and \(\sum_i x_ie_i=0\), while \(\hat y_i=b_0+b_1x_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)e_i &=\sum_i(b_0+b_1x_i-\bar y)e_i\\ &=(b_0-\bar y)\sum_i e_i+b_1\sum_i x_ie_i=0. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{\underbrace{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}_{SS_T} =\underbrace{\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2}_{SS_R} +\underbrace{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}_{SS_{\mathrm{res}}}.} \]

This decomposition holds for ordinary least squares with an intercept.
