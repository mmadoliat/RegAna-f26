Nonconstant Error Variance

Without constant variance,

  • LSEs are still unbiased if linearity and independence of \(x\)s and errors hold.

  • Inference results are not convincing: the usual \(s^2({\bf X'X})^{-1}\) is no longer the right covariance matrix, giving distorted \(p\)-values and incorrect coverage of confidence intervals.

  • The \(b_j\)s are no longer minimum-variance: they have larger standard errors than the weighted least squares estimates (later today).

Is it harder or easier to reject \(H_0: \beta_j = 0\) when the standard error of \(b_j\) is larger?

  • For these consequences to occur, nonconstant error variance must be severe.

Detecting Nonconstant Error Variance

  • Common pattern: the conditional variation of \(y\) to increase with the level of \(y\).

  • It seems natural to plot \(e_i\) against \(y\)

    • \(e_i\) have unequal variances \((\mathrm{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2(1-h_{ii}))\) even when the errors \(\epsilon_i\) have equal variances \(\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2\).
    • \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) are correlated with \(y\) (but uncorrelated with \(\hat{y}\), since \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\bf y}, {\bf e}) = \sigma^2{\bf H}({\bf I}-{\bf H}) = {\bf 0}\)).

  • Use R-student residual plot \(t_i\) vs. \(\hat{y}_i\) because R-student residuals have (approximately) constant variance and they are uncorrelated with \(\hat{y}_i\).

\(r_{ey} = \sqrt{1-R^2}\) https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/5235/what-is-the-expected-correlation-between-residual-and-the-dependent-variable

Residual Plots

  • If the spread of the residuals increases with the level of the fitted values, we might be able to correct the problem by transforming \(y\) down the ladder of power and roots.

Variance Stabilizing Transformation

  • Constant variance assumption is often violated when the response \(y\) follows a distribution whose variance is functionally related to mean.

Can you provide a distribution whose variance is functionally related to its mean?

Relationship of \(\sigma^2\) to \(E(y)\) Transformation
\(\sigma^2 \propto\) constant \(y' = y\) (no transformation)
\(\sigma^2 \propto E(y)\) \(y' = \sqrt{y}\) (square root; Poisson)
\(\sigma^2 \propto E(y)[1-E(y)]\) \(y' = \sin^{-1}(\sqrt{y})\) (arcsin; binomial proportions \(0 \le y_i \le 1\))
\(\sigma^2 \propto [E(y)]^2\) \(y' = \ln(y)\) (natural log)
\(\sigma^2 \propto [E(y)]^3\) \(y' = y^{-1/2}\) (reciprocal square root)
\(\sigma^2 \propto [E(y)]^4\) \(y' = y^{-1}\) (reciprocal)
  • Answer to the question: Poisson counts (\(\mathrm{Var}= \mu\)), binomial proportions (\(\mathrm{Var}= \mu(1-\mu)/m\)), gamma/exponential (\(\mathrm{Var}\propto \mu^2\)).
  • If not corrected, the regression coefficients will have larger standard errors (less precision).
  • The strength of a transformation depends on the amount of curvature that it induces
  • a mild transformation applied over a relatively narrow range of values (e.g., y max / y min < 2, 3) has little effect. On the other hand, a strong transformation over a wide range of values will have a dramatic effect on the analysis.

Where Does the Table Come From?

  • If \(E(y) = \mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(y) = \sigma^2(\mu)\), a first-order Taylor expansion (delta method) gives \[\mathrm{Var}\big(g(y)\big) \approx [g'(\mu)]^2\, \sigma^2(\mu).\]
  • Choose \(g\) with \(g'(\mu) \propto 1/\sigma(\mu)\) to make the variance constant.
  • \(\sigma^2 \propto \mu \Rightarrow g' \propto \mu^{-1/2} \Rightarrow g(y) = \sqrt{y}\)
  • \(\sigma^2 \propto \mu^2 \Rightarrow g' \propto \mu^{-1} \Rightarrow g(y) = \ln y\)
  • In general, \(\sigma \propto \mu^{\alpha} \Rightarrow g(y) = y^{1-\alpha}\), i.e., \(\lambda = 1 - \alpha\) (\(\lambda = 0\) means \(\ln y\)).

Keep \(\lambda = 1 - \alpha\) in mind: it is exactly the rule behind Tukey’s spread-level plot later today.

  • Check one more row: \(\sigma^2 \propto \mu^4 \Rightarrow \alpha = 2 \Rightarrow \lambda = -1\) (reciprocal).
  • Arcsine row: \(g'(\mu) \propto 1/\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}\) integrates to \(\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\mu}\) (up to a constant).

Tukey’s Spread-Level Plot

  • Plot log of the absolute R-Student residuals vs. log of the (positive) fitted values.
  • The fitted line slope \(b\) estimates \(\alpha\) in \(\sigma \propto \mu^\alpha\), so it suggests a variance-stabilizing power transformation \(\lambda = 1 - b\) for \(y\).
car::spreadLevelPlot(ciafit, smooth = FALSE)

spreadLevelPlot(logciafit, smooth = FALSE)

  • infant as \(y\): suggested power is roughly \(0.4\) (cases with negative fitted values are dropped).

  • log(infant) as \(y\): suggested power is close to \(1\), so no further transformation is needed.

  • Plot log of the absolute R-Student residuals vs. log of the (positive) fitted values

  • Fit by robust regression to generate fitted lines.

  • The slope \(b\) of the fitted line suggests a variance-stabilizing power transformation \(\lambda = 1 - b\) for \(y\).

  • Why: if \(\sigma \propto \mu^\alpha\), then \(\log|t_i| \approx c + \alpha \log \hat{y}_i\); the delta-method slide gives \(\lambda = 1 - \alpha\).

  • With 14 negative fitted values dropped for the raw model, the suggested power should be read as rough guidance.

Weighted Least Squares (WLS)

  • Suppose that the other assumptions hold but \(\epsilon_i \stackrel{indep}{\sim} N(0, \sigma_i^2)\): with \(n\) different variances, the model isn’t estimable.

  • If \(\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2/w_i\) for known \(w_i\), minimize the Weighted Residual Sum of Squares. In simple linear regression, \[S(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^nw_i\epsilon_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i \right)^2\]

  • Setting \(\partial S/\partial\beta_0 = \partial S/\partial\beta_1 = 0\) gives the normal equations \[\begin{align} \beta_0\sum_{i=1}^n w_i+\beta_1\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i &= \sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i \\ \beta_0 \sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i + \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_iy_i \end{align}\]

Observations with large variances will have smaller weights.

\[(b_0, b_1, \dots, b_k) = \underset{{\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k}}{\mathrm{arg \, min}} S(\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k)\]

\[S(\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k) = \sum_{i=1}^nw_i\epsilon_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^nw_i\left(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^k\beta_j x_{ij}\right)^2\]

Weighted Least Squares in Matrix Form

  • In MLR, with \({\bf W} = \mathrm{diag}(w_1, \dots, w_n)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\epsilon) = \sigma^2{\bf W}^{-1}\), minimize \[S(\boldsymbol\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i\left(y_i - {\bf x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta\right)^2 = ({\bf y} - {\bf X}\boldsymbol\beta)'{\bf W}({\bf y} - {\bf X}\boldsymbol\beta)\]
  • Normal equations \({\bf (X'WX)}\boldsymbol\beta = {\bf X'Wy}\) (the SLR equations are the case \(k = 1\)), so \[{\bf b}_W = ({\bf X'WX})^{-1}{\bf X'Wy}\]
  • WLS = OLS on rescaled data: \({\bf B} = {\bf W}^{1/2}{\bf X}\), \({\bf z} = {\bf W}^{1/2}{\bf y}\) (row \(i\) times \(\sqrt{w_i}\)) gives \(\mathrm{Var}({\bf W}^{1/2}\boldsymbol\epsilon) = \sigma^2{\bf I}\) and \({\bf b}_W = ({\bf B'B})^{-1}{\bf B'z}\).
  • Generalized LS: for \(\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\epsilon) = \sigma^2{\bf V}\) (possibly correlated errors), \({\bf b}_{GLS} = ({\bf X'V^{-1}X})^{-1}{\bf X'V^{-1}y}\). WLS is the special case \({\bf V} = {\bf W}^{-1}\).
  • The rescaling view explains why, after a WLS fit, we check the weighted residuals \(\sqrt{w_i}e_i\) against \(\sqrt{w_i}\hat{y}_i\): they behave like OLS residuals.
  • GLS derivation (LRA 5.5.1): write \({\bf V} = {\bf KK}\) with \({\bf K}\) symmetric, transform \({\bf z} = {\bf K}^{-1}{\bf y}\), \({\bf B} = {\bf K}^{-1}{\bf X}\), and apply OLS.
  • In R: lm(y ~ x, weights = w) for WLS; nlme::gls() for general \({\bf V}\).

Methods of Choosing Weights

  • Prior knowledge or information

  • Residual analysis

    • \(\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i) \propto x_{i}\), suggest \(w_i = 1/x_{i}\)
    • When \(y_i\) is an average of \(n_i\) observations at \(x_i\), \(\mathrm{Var}(y_i) =\sigma^2 / n_{i}\) and suggest \(w_i = n_i\)

  • Chosen inversely proportional to variances of measurement error

  • Guess at the weights and iteratively re-estimate them (feasible GLS)

If WLS should have been used but OLS is used instead, the OLS estimator is still unbiased, but it is no longer minimum-variance, and its usual standard errors are wrong.

Summary: Nonconstant Variance

  1. Detect: R-student residuals vs. \(\hat{y}\) (funnel), spread-level plot, car::ncvTest().
  2. Variance a function of the mean: variance-stabilizing transformation (table, \(\lambda = 1 - b\) from the spread-level plot, or Box-Cox).
  3. Variance pattern known or estimable (e.g., near-replicates): WLS with \(w_i \propto 1/\hat{\sigma}_i^2\).
  4. Recheck the residuals (weighted residuals for WLS).
  5. Alternative: keep OLS with robust standard errors or the bootstrap.
