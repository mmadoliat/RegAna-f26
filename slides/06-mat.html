Matrix Algebra for Regression

\[y_i= \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_kx_{ik} + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2), \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, n\]

\[\bf y = \bf X \boldsymbol \beta+ \boldsymbol \epsilon\] where

\[\begin{align} \bf y = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix},\quad \bf X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1k} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{nk} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol \beta= \begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_k \end{bmatrix} , \quad \boldsymbol \epsilon= \begin{bmatrix} \epsilon_1 \\ \epsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \epsilon_n\end{bmatrix} \end{align}\]

The main goal is not matrix computation by hand. It is to understand the geometry and algebra behind

\[ {\bf b}=({\bf X}'{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}'{\bf y}. \]

Then

Linear Combinations

A linear combination of vectors \({\bf v}_1,\dots,{\bf v}_k\) is

\[ c_1{\bf v}_1+\cdots+c_k{\bf v}_k. \]

For example,

\[ 2 \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Matrix multiplication \({\bf Xb}\) is a linear combination of the columns of \({\bf X}\).

If

\[ {\bf X}= \begin{bmatrix} {\bf x}_1 & {\bf x}_2 & \cdots & {\bf x}_p \end{bmatrix}, \]

then

\[ {\bf Xb} = b_1{\bf x}_1+\cdots+b_p{\bf x}_p. \]

Span and Column Space

The span of vectors \({\bf v}_1,\ldots,{\bf v}_k\) is the set of all their linear combinations:

\[ \operatorname{span}({\bf v}_1,\dots,{\bf v}_k) = \left\{ c_1{\bf v}_1+\cdots+c_k{\bf v}_k \right\}. \]

For a matrix

\[ {\bf X}= \begin{bmatrix} {\bf x}_1 & \cdots & {\bf x}_p \end{bmatrix}, \]

the column space is

\[ \operatorname{Col}({\bf X}) = \operatorname{span}({\bf x}_1,\dots,{\bf x}_p). \]

In regression, every fitted-value vector

\[ \hat{\bf y}={\bf Xb} \]

must lie in \(\operatorname{Col}({\bf X})\).

Why Column Space Matters for Regression

Suppose

\[ {\bf X} = \begin{bmatrix} {\bf 1} & {\bf x}_1 & {\bf x}_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then every possible fitted vector has the form

\[ {\bf Xb} = b_0{\bf 1}+b_1{\bf x}_1+b_2{\bf x}_2. \]

  • Changing \({\bf b}\) moves us to a different point in \(\operatorname{Col}({\bf X})\).
  • Least squares chooses the point in \(\operatorname{Col}({\bf X})\) closest to \({\bf y}\).
  • This geometric viewpoint will explain the normal equations later.

  • Let \({\bf A}\) be an \(n \times m\) matrix. For a square matrix,

\[ \boxed{\det({\bf A})\ne0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad {\bf A}\text{ is invertible}} \]

  • Geometrically, the absolute determinant gives the factor by which the linear transformation scales area in 2D or volume in higher dimensions.
  • A zero determinant means that the transformation collapses the space into a lower-dimensional subspace.

Determinant: Geometric Interpretation

For a \(2\times2\) matrix, \(|\det({\bf A})|\) is the factor by which areas are scaled.

  • \(|\det({\bf A})|>1\): area expands.
  • \(0<|\det({\bf A})|<1\): area contracts.
  • \(\det({\bf A})<0\): orientation is reversed.
  • \(\det({\bf A})=0\): the plane is collapsed to a line or a point.

If \(\det({\bf A})=0\), some column information is redundant.
That is exactly the kind of issue that appears with perfect multicollinearity.

Linear Independence

  • Vectors \({\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2, \dots, {\bf v}_k\) is said to be linearly independent if for scalars \(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k} \in \mathbf{R},\) \[c_{1}{\bf v}_1 + c_{2}{\bf v}_2 + \cdots + c_{k}{\bf v}_k = \bf 0\] can only be satisfied by \(c_i = 0\) for all \(i = 1, 2, \dots, k\).

Are \({\bf v}_1 = (1, 1)'\) and \({\bf v}_2 = (-3, 2)'\) linearly independent?

Ways to check linear independence:

  • solve the homogeneous linear system \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\).

  • check if \(\text{det} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \right)\) is non-zero.

Rank

  • The rank of a matrix \({\bf A} = \begin{bmatrix} {\bf a}_1 & {\bf a}_2 & \cdots & {\bf a}_m \end{bmatrix}\) is the number of linearly independent columns (dimension of the column space).

  • If \(k\) of the \(m\) column vectors \({\bf a}_1, {\bf a}_2, \dots, {\bf a}_m\) are linearly independent, the rank of \({\bf A}\) is \(k\).

  • The remaining \(m-k\) columns of \({\bf A}\) can be written as a linear combination of the \(k\) linearly independent columns.

  • If \(k = m\), it is full rank
  • If \(k\) of the \(n\) column vectors \({\bf a}_1, {\bf a}_2, \dots, {\bf a}_n\) are linearly independent, the rank of \({\bf A}\) is \(k\). The remaining \(n-k\) columns can be written as a linear combination of the \(k\) columns.
  • The vectors \({\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2, \dots, {\bf v}_k\) is said to be linearly dependent if there exist scalars \(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k},\) not all zero, such that \[c_{1}{\bf v}_1 + c_{2}{\bf v}_2 + \cdots + c_{k}{\bf v}_k = \bf 0\]
  • The column vectors \({\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2, \dots, {\bf v}_k\) is said to be linearly independent if it is not linearly dependent, that is, if \[c_{1}{\bf v}_1 + c_{2}{\bf v}_2 + \cdots + c_{k}{\bf v}_k = \bf 0\] can only be satisfied by \(c_i = 0, i = 1, 2, \dots, k.\)

Linear Systems: \({\bf Ax}={\bf b}\)

A system of linear equations can be written compactly as

\[ {\bf Ax}={\bf b}. \]

Example:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x_1+x_2 &=5,\\ x_1+3x_2 &=7. \end{aligned} \]

becomes

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5\\7 \end{bmatrix}. \]

A_sys <- matrix(c(2, 1,
                  1, 3), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
b_sys <- c(5, 7)

solve(A_sys, b_sys)
[1] 1.6 1.8

Unique Solutions and Rank

For a square matrix \({\bf A}_{n\times n}\), the following statements are equivalent:

  • \({\bf A}\) has full rank \(n\).
  • The columns of \({\bf A}\) are linearly independent.
  • \(\det({\bf A})\ne0\).
  • \({\bf A}^{-1}\) exists.
  • \({\bf Ax}={\bf b}\) has a unique solution for every \({\bf b}\).

Regression connection:

\[ {\bf b}=({\bf X}'{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}'{\bf y} \]

requires \({\bf X}\) to have full column rank.

Operations: Multiplication

Geometry of Vectors

Inner Product (Dot Product)

For vectors \({\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathbb R^n\),

\[ {\bf x}'{\bf y} = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i. \]

x <- c(1, 2, 3)
y <- c(4, -1, 2)

sum(x * y)
[1] 8
t(x) %*% y
     [,1]
[1,]    8

The dot product measures how strongly two vectors point in the same direction.

Outer Product

For column vectors \({\bf x}\in\mathbb R^m\) and \({\bf y}\in\mathbb R^n\),

\[ {\bf x}{\bf y}' \]

is an \(m\times n\) matrix.

For example,

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}3&4&5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3&4&5\\ 6&8&10 \end{bmatrix}. \]

x <- c(1, 2)
y <- c(3, 4, 5)

x %o% y
     [,1] [,2] [,3]
[1,]    3    4    5
[2,]    6    8   10
  • Contrast the inner product x’y, which is a scalar, with the outer product xy’, which is a matrix.
  • Outer products appear naturally in covariance matrices and matrix decompositions.

Length and Distance

The Euclidean norm of \({\bf x}\) is \(\|{\bf x}\|=\sqrt{{\bf x}'{\bf x}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.\)

The distance between \({\bf x}\) and \({\bf y}\) is \(\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|.\)

x <- c(1, 2)
y <- c(4, 6)

sqrt(sum(x^2))
[1] 2.2
sqrt(sum((x - y)^2))
[1] 5

Least squares minimizes a squared Euclidean distance:

\[ \|{\bf y}-{\bf Xb}\|^2. \]

Angle Between Vectors

The angle \(\theta\) between nonzero vectors \({\bf x}\) and \({\bf y}\) satisfies

\[ \cos(\theta) = \frac{{\bf x}'{\bf y}} {\|{\bf x}\|\,\|{\bf y}\|}. \]

x <- c(1, 0)
y <- c(1, 1)

theta <- acos(
  sum(x * y) /
    (sqrt(sum(x^2)) * sqrt(sum(y^2)))
)

theta * 180 / pi
[1] 45
  • \(\theta=0^\circ\): same direction
  • \(\theta=90^\circ\): orthogonal
  • \(\theta=180^\circ\): opposite directions

Orthogonality

Two vectors are orthogonal if

\[ {\bf x}'{\bf y}=0. \]

x <- c(1, 2)
y <- c(2, -1)

t(x) %*% y
     [,1]
[1,]    0

In least squares,

\[ {\bf X}'{\bf e}={\bf 0}. \]

The residual vector is orthogonal to every column of \({\bf X}\).

Projection onto a Vector

The projection of \({\bf y}\) onto a nonzero vector \({\bf x}\) is

\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\bf x}({\bf y}) = \frac{{\bf x}'{\bf y}}{{\bf x}'{\bf x}}{\bf x}. \]

x <- c(1, 2)
y <- c(4, 1)

proj_y <- as.numeric(t(x) %*% y / as.numeric(t(x) %*% x)) * x
proj_y
[1] 1.2 2.4
# residual from projection
e <- y - proj_y
t(x) %*% e
     [,1]
[1,]    0
  • The residual from the projection is orthogonal to x.
  • This is the one-dimensional version of least squares projection onto the column space of X.

Special Matrices: Symmetric and Identity

  • A square matrix \((m = n)\) \({\bf A}_{n \times n}\) is a symmetric matrix if \({\bf A = A}'\).

\[{\bf A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \\ \end{bmatrix} \quad {\bf A}' =\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • A square matrix \({\bf I}_{n \times n}\) whose diagonal elements are 1’s and off diagonal elements are 0’s is called an identity matrix of order \(n\). \[{\bf I} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]
  • If the inverse exists, it is unique.

Inverse vs. Solving a System in R

Mathematically, \({\bf x}={\bf A}^{-1}{\bf b}\) solves \({\bf Ax}={\bf b}.\)

In computation, it is usually better to solve the system directly:

A <- matrix(c(4, 1,
              1, 3), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
b <- c(1, 2)

# preferred
solve(A, b)
[1] 0.091 0.636
# mathematically equivalent
solve(A) %*% b
      [,1]
[1,] 0.091
[2,] 0.636

Use solve(A, b) when you want the solution to \({\bf Ax}={\bf b}\).

Avoid forming an inverse explicitly unless you actually need the inverse matrix.

Idempotent, Orthogonal, Positive Definite

  • A square matrix \({\bf A}\) is idempotent if \({\bf A}{\bf A} = {\bf A}\).
  • A square matrix \({\bf A}\) is orthogonal if \({\bf A}^{-1} = {\bf A}'\) and hence \({\bf A}'{\bf A} = {\bf I}\).
  • Let \({\bf A}\) be a \(n \times n\) matrix and \({\bf y}\) be a \(n \times 1\) vector. \[{\bf y}'{\bf A} {\bf y} = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}y_iy_j\] is called a quadratic form of \({\bf y}\).
  • A symmetric matrix \({\bf A}\) is said to be
    • positive definite if \({\bf y}'{\bf A} {\bf y} > 0\) for all \({\bf y \ne 0}\).
    • positive semi-definite if \({\bf y}'{\bf A} {\bf y} \ge 0\) for all \({\bf y \ne 0}\) and \({\bf y}'{\bf A} {\bf y} = 0\) for some \({\bf y \ne 0}\).
  • If \({\bf A}\) is symmetric and idempotent, then \({\bf A}\) is positive semi-definite.

Positive Definite Matrices and Regression

If \({\bf X}\) has full column rank, then for every nonzero vector \({\bf a}\),

\[ {\bf a}'{\bf X}'{\bf X}{\bf a} = ({\bf Xa})'({\bf Xa}) = \|{\bf Xa}\|^2 >0. \]

Therefore, \(\boxed{{\bf X}'{\bf X}\text{ is positive definite}}\) and hence invertible.

If the columns of \({\bf X}\) are linearly dependent, there is a nonzero \({\bf a}\) with \({\bf Xa}={\bf 0},\)

so

\[ {\bf a}'{\bf X}'{\bf X}{\bf a}=0, \]

and \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\) is not positive definite.

This is another way to understand why perfect multicollinearity prevents unique least-squares coefficients.

Useful Properties of the Trace

For conformable matrices,

\[ \operatorname{tr}({\bf A}+{\bf B}) = \operatorname{tr}({\bf A}) + \operatorname{tr}({\bf B}), \]

\[ \operatorname{tr}(c{\bf A}) = c\,\operatorname{tr}({\bf A}), \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}({\bf AB}) = \operatorname{tr}({\bf BA}) }. \]

For a symmetric matrix,

\[ \operatorname{tr}({\bf A})=\sum_j \lambda_j,\quad \textrm{and}\quad \operatorname{det}({\bf A})=\prod_j \lambda_j. \]

where \(\lambda_j\)’s are eigenvalues of \({\bf A}.\)

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

  • For a square matrix \({\bf A}\), if we can find a scalar \(\lambda\) and a non-zero vector \({\bf x}\) such that \[{\bf Ax} = \lambda {\bf x},\]
    • \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \({\bf A}\)
    • \({\bf x}\) is a corresponding eigenvector of \({\bf A}\).
  • A general \(n\times n\) matrix has \(n\) eigenvalues over the complex numbers when counted with multiplicity, but it may not have \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors.
  • A real symmetric matrix has \(n\) real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

Geometric Meaning of Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Eigen-decomposition

  • Eigen-decomposition: Any symmetric matrix \({\bf A}_{n \times n}\) can be decomposed as \[{\bf A} = {\bf V\boldsymbol \Lambda V'}\]

  • \(\boldsymbol \Lambda\) is a \(n \times n\) diagnonal matrix whose elements are eigenvalues \(\lambda_j\) of \({\bf A}\) \[\boldsymbol \Lambda= \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}\]

  • \({\bf V} = [{\bf v}_1 \quad {\bf v}_2 \quad \dots \quad {\bf v}_n]\) is a \(n \times n\) orthogonal matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors of \({\bf A}.\)

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Let \({\bf A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) be a square matrix with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\).

  • \(\operatorname{tr}({\bf A}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\)
  • \(\det({\bf A}) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\)
  • If \({\bf A}\) is symmetric, \(\operatorname{rank}({\bf A}) = \#\{i : \lambda_i \neq 0\}\)
  • If \({\bf A}\) is symmetric, all \(\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}\)

Eigenvalues and Positive Definiteness

For a real symmetric matrix \({\bf A}\):

  • \({\bf A}\) is positive definite iff all eigenvalues are positive.
  • \({\bf A}\) is positive semi-definite iff all eigenvalues are nonnegative.
  • \({\bf A}\) is singular iff at least one eigenvalue is zero.

For regression, small eigenvalues of \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\) indicate nearly dependent predictor directions and therefore potential multicollinearity.

Eigenvalues of \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\)

set.seed(123)

x1 <- rnorm(100)
x2 <- x1 + rnorm(100, sd = 0.05)

X_col <- cbind(1, x1, x2)

eigen(t(X_col) %*% X_col)$values
[1] 168.45  97.75   0.12

Compare with less-correlated predictors:

x3 <- rnorm(100)
X_ok <- cbind(1, x1, x3)

eigen(t(X_ok) %*% X_ok)$values
[1] 108  96  69
  • When two columns are almost linearly dependent, one direction in X’X has very little variation.
  • That appears as a small eigenvalue.
  • We will revisit this idea when we discuss multicollinearity.

Matrix Decompositions

Why Matrix Decompositions?

A matrix decomposition writes a matrix as a product of simpler matrices.

They are useful for

  • solving systems of equations,
  • obtaining numerically stable computations,
  • understanding the structure and rank of a matrix,
  • obtaining low-rank approximations.

We will briefly connect four decompositions to regression:

  1. eigenvalue decomposition,
  2. Cholesky decomposition,
  3. QR decomposition,
  4. singular value decomposition.
  • The goal is not to derive every algorithm.
  • Students should recognize what each decomposition does and why regression software uses such decompositions.

Cholesky Decomposition

For a symmetric positive-definite matrix \({\bf A}\),

\[ \boxed{ {\bf A}={\bf L}{\bf L}' } \]

where \({\bf L}\) is lower triangular.

Because \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\) is positive definite when \({\bf X}\) has full column rank, Cholesky decomposition can be used to solve systems involving \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\).

A_chol <- matrix(c(4, 2,
                   2, 3), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)

R <- chol(A_chol)
R
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    2  1.0
[2,]    0  1.4
t(R) %*% R
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    4    2
[2,]    2    3
  • R returns the upper-triangular factor R such that A = R’R.
  • Cholesky is useful for positive-definite systems, including covariance matrices.

QR Decomposition

For a tall matrix \({\bf X}\) with \(n\ge p\), \(\boxed{{\bf X}={\bf Q}{\bf R}}\), where

  • \({\bf Q}'{\bf Q}={\bf I}\), so the columns of \({\bf Q}\) are orthonormal;
  • \({\bf R}\) is upper triangular.
X_qr <- matrix(c(1, 1,
                 1, 2,
                 1, 3,
                 1, 4), nrow = 4, byrow = TRUE)
qrX <- qr(X_qr); Q <- qr.Q(qrX); R <- qr.R(qrX)
Q %*% R
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    1    1
[2,]    1    2
[3,]    1    3
[4,]    1    4

QR decomposition is especially important for least squares because it lets us solve the regression problem without explicitly forming \(({\bf X}'{\bf X})^{-1}\).

Least Squares Using QR

Suppose \({\bf X}={\bf Q}{\bf R}.\) Then the least-squares problem \[\min_{\bf b}\|{\bf y}-{\bf Xb}\|^2\]

becomes \[\min_{\bf b}\|{\bf y}-{\bf QRb}\|^2.\]

Using the orthogonality of \({\bf Q}\) leads to the triangular system \(\boxed{{\bf Rb}={\bf Q}'{\bf y}}.\)

y_qr <- c(2, 3, 5, 8)
(b_qr <- backsolve(R, t(Q) %*% y_qr))
     [,1]
[1,] -0.5
[2,]  2.0
coef(lm(y_qr ~ X_qr[, 2]))
(Intercept)   X_qr[, 2] 
       -0.5         2.0
  • This is computationally preferable to explicitly computing (X’X)^(-1).
  • The details of the numerical algorithm are not needed here; the conceptual point is that orthogonalization simplifies least squares.

Singular Value Decomposition

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Every \(n\times p\) matrix \({\bf X}\) can be written as

\[ \boxed{ {\bf X}={\bf U}{\bf D}{\bf V}' } \]

where

  • \({\bf U}\) has orthonormal columns,
  • \({\bf V}\) has orthonormal columns,
  • \({\bf D}\) is diagonal with nonnegative singular values

\[ d_1\ge d_2\ge\cdots\ge0. \]

Unlike eigendecomposition, the SVD applies to rectangular matrices.

SVD in R

X_demo <- matrix(c(1, 2,
                   2, 1,
                   3, 4), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
sv <- svd(X_demo)
sv$d; sv$u; sv$v
[1] 5.8 1.1
      [,1]  [,2]
[1,] -0.38  0.45
[2,] -0.35 -0.88
[3,] -0.86  0.16
      [,1]  [,2]
[1,] -0.63 -0.78
[2,] -0.78  0.63

Reconstruct \({\bf X}\):

sv$u %*% diag(sv$d) %*% t(sv$v)
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    1    2
[2,]    2    1
[3,]    3    4

Rank and Singular Values

The rank of a matrix equals the number of nonzero singular values:

\[ \operatorname{rank}({\bf X}) = \#\{j:d_j>0\}. \]

If a singular value is very small rather than exactly zero, the matrix is nearly rank deficient.

Regression connection:

  • zero singular value \(\rightarrow\) perfect multicollinearity;
  • very small singular value \(\rightarrow\) near multicollinearity and unstable coefficient estimates.

Connecting SVD and \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\)

If \({\bf X}={\bf U}{\bf D}{\bf V}'\), then \[{\bf X}'{\bf X}={\bf V}{\bf D}^2{\bf V}'.\]

Therefore, \[\lambda_j({\bf X}'{\bf X})=d_j^2.\]

The eigenvalues of \({\bf X}'{\bf X}\) are the squared singular values of \({\bf X}\).

This connects

  • rank,
  • multicollinearity,
  • eigendecomposition,
  • SVD,
  • and least squares.
